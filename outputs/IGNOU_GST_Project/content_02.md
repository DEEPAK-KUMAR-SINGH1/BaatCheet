# Chapter 2 — Review of Literature
## 2.1 Review approach and source quality
The literature review is organised by the question a source can answer. Conceptual research explains the design of a tax system; fiscal research studies revenue performance; administrative guidance describes procedures; and surveys record the views of the respondents reached by their investigators. None of these categories can substitute automatically for the others. Their value depends on the claim being examined.
The supplied synopsis names several journal articles, but complete and reliable publication records were not established for four of those items during preparation. They are therefore not relied upon as verified evidence in this report. The identifiable 2009 paper has been cited using its verified authorship and institutional publisher: Poddar and Ahmad, Department of Economic Affairs. This corrects the attribution in the synopsis without changing the project’s objectives.
The review gives priority to materials available from the original institution. It uses the official text of legislation for the credit framework, GSTN material for return procedures, government releases for statistical observations and original research publications for analytical findings. Published industry surveys are attributed to their authors and identified as secondary evidence for this project.
This is a structured narrative review, not a claimed exhaustive systematic review. No fabricated screening count or database search total is reported. The purpose is to build an auditable argument from a focused body of relevant sources. Where a source has a limited population or an earlier reference period, that limitation travels with the finding rather than disappearing when the finding enters the report.

## 2.2 Tax design and the credit chain
Poddar and Ahmad (2009) examine GST reform and intergovernmental considerations in India. Their discussion provides a pre-implementation perspective on problems such as cascading and the relationship between the tax base and federal arrangements. The paper is useful for understanding reform objectives; it cannot be evidence of outcomes experienced by SMEs after 2017, because it predates implementation.
The distinction between an expected mechanism and an observed result is essential. A design may permit a more continuous credit chain, but the extent to which a firm benefits depends on its transactions and the conditions governing eligibility. The present report uses the earlier paper as a conceptual starting point and tests its relevance against later administrative and statistical material. It does not describe its expectations as measured achievements.
From an enterprise perspective, the credit-chain argument suggests three separate questions. Does the firm incur tax on purchases that can be eligible for credit? Can it document and use that credit appropriately? Does the resulting commercial advantage remain with the firm or pass to customers through prices? Answering only the first question would overstate the benefit available to the enterprise.
The literature also encourages a distinction between a broad-based tax and a single uniform outcome. A coordinated system can contain different rates, exemptions and procedural requirements. For business analysis, the relevant comparison is often between the effective treatment of a specific supply chain before and after a change. Such a comparison requires more detailed evidence than a national reform narrative can provide.

## 2.3 Evidence on revenue performance
Mukherjee (2023), in NIPFP Working Paper 392, compares pre- and post-GST revenue streams and studies revenue performance, collection efficiency and related gaps. The paper reports improvement in tax buoyancy while drawing attention to the care needed in comparing tax-to-GDP measures. It also treats economic disruption and the composition of revenue as relevant to interpretation. These are fiscal findings, not estimates of the return earned by an individual enterprise.
The main lesson for this project is methodological. Comparing monetary totals across time requires attention to prices, the tax base and the scope of the measure. A gross collection series includes amounts that may subsequently be refunded. A series including taxes on imports describes a broader base than domestic collections alone. Failure to distinguish these categories can create a convincing chart with an unclear economic meaning.
The present study accordingly uses a tightly defined two-year official comparison rather than combining partially comparable numbers from multiple summaries. It shows gross revenue, refunds and net revenue together. Its discussion asks what the movements establish about the aggregate tax system and why they cannot identify the distribution of costs and benefits across SMEs.
A further implication is that national fiscal improvement and enterprise difficulty can coexist. Higher aggregate revenue does not disprove a small exporter’s liquidity problem, just as a particular firm’s difficulty does not establish that the tax system is failing nationally. The appropriate unit of analysis must remain visible. Chapter 4 uses this distinction when moving from official collection data to the enterprise-level mechanisms discussed in the synopsis.
===
## 2.4 Compliance costs and firm size
Mukherjee and Badola (2022) examine VAT compliance costs of unincorporated enterprises using pre-GST NSSO data for 2015–16. Their work is relevant to measurement: it identifies the difficulty of separating ordinary business expenses from tax-compliance expenses. It also notes incomplete coverage of costs and potential offsetting benefits. Because the underlying data precede GST, the study must not be presented as an estimate of current GST compliance costs.
For this project, the important question is how a cost should be classified. An accounting package may serve stock control, customer billing and tax reporting at the same time. Allocating its entire subscription price to GST would overstate the incremental tax burden. Allocating none of it would miss the resources needed for compliance. A defensible estimate needs an explicit allocation rule.
The same issue applies to labour. A salaried employee may spend only part of the month on tax work. An owner may perform that work without recording any wage expense. Cash expenditure and economic cost are therefore different measures. A study that reports only accountant fees may be useful, but it is not measuring the complete resource burden.
The report uses a hypothetical fixed-cost calculation to explain scale effects without claiming an observed average. The calculation holds annual cost constant and changes turnover. It shows why identical expenditure can represent a much larger share of sales for a smaller firm. This arithmetic supports an analytical proposition about proportional burden; it does not estimate how much Indian SMEs actually spend or how that spending changed after GST.

## 2.5 Transition, credit and liquidity
The Reserve Bank of India’s February 2018 circular provided specified relief for qualifying MSME borrowers registered under GST. The circular is evidence of an institutional response during the transition period, subject to its stated conditions. It is not a current blanket relaxation and is not used here to infer that every MSME experienced financial distress (RBI, 2018).
Transition evidence needs to be read in time. A business learning a new process may incur conversion costs that later diminish, while recurring reconciliation tasks may persist. The first year of implementation can therefore be a poor estimate of mature-system costs. Conversely, later stability should not be used to deny that adjustment costs were real when firms encountered them.
Liquidity analysis also requires precision. A tax-related cash gap may arise from the timing of receivables, input credit or refunds. Ordinary slow-moving inventory and customer default can create similar pressure. A report must identify the additional mechanism it attributes to GST rather than assigning the firm’s entire working-capital requirement to taxation.
This study addresses that problem through an explicitly assumed financing example. The example begins with a specified amount and delay, then calculates the cost at an assumed annual interest rate. It demonstrates how timing matters without claiming to measure national losses. The result is interpreted as a sensitivity calculation: a longer delay raises financing cost when all other assumptions remain unchanged. The actual effect on a firm would depend on its credit terms, cash balances and ability to borrow.
===
## 2.6 Published industry surveys
Deloitte’s GST@8 publication reports a survey of 963 responses across eight industries from senior executives. Its published summary states that 85% of respondents held a positive view of GST in 2025, compared with reported positive sentiment of 59% in 2022, 72% in 2023 and 84% in 2024. These are findings of the original survey, used here as secondary information (Deloitte, 2025a).
The survey provides a useful form of evidence: reported experience can reveal whether businesses value a process and which changes they seek. It is different from an official tax collection series because its measurement concerns opinion rather than revenue. It is also different from a financial-statement analysis because a positive assessment does not quantify the respondent’s profit or cash flow.
The interpretation must respect coverage. Senior executives participating in an industry survey are not automatically representative of all owner-managed micro enterprises in India. Without a probability sampling framework and appropriate weights, the overall result cannot be converted into a national SME approval rate. Likewise, an overall percentage should not be attributed to the MSME subgroup unless that subgroup result is separately reported.
The year-to-year comparison has another boundary. Published results from successive editions may involve different respondents and composition. A rising percentage does not establish that the same firms became more positive. Chapter 4 presents the reported series with this qualification and uses it alongside administrative and conceptual evidence. The value lies in triangulation, not in treating one survey percentage as a verdict on the reform.

## 2.7 Technology and administrative guidance
GSTN’s QRMP guidance explains an arrangement under which eligible taxpayers can file specified returns quarterly while making monthly tax payments. The official guidance identifies a turnover ceiling of Rs 5 crore and other conditions. It is a concrete example of administrative differentiation intended to make the process more manageable for eligible taxpayers (GSTN, n.d.).
Guidance establishes what a scheme is designed to do; it does not prove the number of hours it saves. A taxpayer still needs sufficiently current information to determine obligations and support customer-facing records. The analytical question is therefore whether the scheme reduces the frequency of a specific filing task, the total workload, or both. These should not be assumed to be identical.
Notification 10/2023-Central Tax extended the e-invoicing threshold to aggregate turnover exceeding Rs 5 crore from 1 August 2023, subject to the applicable framework. This helps correct the synopsis’s broad wording that might imply all SMEs must generate statutory e-invoices. The requirement is threshold- and category-dependent (CBIC, 2023).
Technology can improve consistency when it removes duplicate entry or highlights exceptions. It can also expose weak master data more quickly. A wrong customer identifier copied automatically into several systems is still wrong. The report therefore treats digital capability as a combination of usable tools, trained people and clear review responsibility. Software adoption alone is not used as evidence of accurate compliance or higher productivity. The relevant outcome is whether reliable information reaches the right process at the right time.

An important gap remains between institutional design and realised financial outcomes. The report addresses the gap through transparent analytical reasoning and labelled examples, while leaving empirical estimates to future research with appropriate business-level observations.

## 2.9 Synthesis and unresolved questions
The reviewed material supports a distinction between formal simplification and practical effort. A broader credit system can remove some discontinuities, yet each firm must still supply reliable transaction information. The amount of work depends on transaction volume, the quality of the starting records and how often exceptions arise. The report’s synthesis therefore focuses on the conditions that connect a policy feature to a business outcome.
Three unresolved questions are particularly relevant. First, how much of an enterprise’s accounting expenditure is additional tax-compliance cost? Second, how much of an observed cash shortage is caused by tax timing rather than ordinary commercial delay? Third, which firms gain access to customers because of formal records, and what other qualifications do those customers require? The sources reviewed do not provide a representative common dataset that answers all three.
The unresolved questions also explain why apparently conflicting accounts can be credible. An organised B2B supplier may value the documentation chain, while a small consumer-facing enterprise experiences mainly the administrative workload. A rapidly growing firm may absorb a fixed cost more easily than a stable low-turnover firm. Different findings may reflect different business populations rather than errors by the researchers.
The appropriate synthesis is therefore conditional and comparative. It identifies recurring mechanisms, states the circumstances under which they matter and avoids assigning a universal magnitude to them. This approach leaves room for disagreement in the evidence without treating every claim as equally reliable. A dated official statistic remains stronger evidence for a national total than an unattributed assertion, even though neither alone answers every question about business performance.

## 2.10 Research gap and analytical propositions
The main gap is the absence, within the evidence assembled for this project, of a representative linked dataset containing firm size, tax status, compliance expenditure, credit utilisation, payment timing and financial outcomes over comparable periods. Without that structure, it is difficult to distinguish the effect of GST from changes in demand, prices, technology or financing conditions.
The report uses four analytical propositions to organise its discussion. The first is that a fixed administrative cost will represent a larger proportion of turnover for a smaller business, all else equal. This is an arithmetic proposition. The second is that a credit mechanism is commercially useful only when the credit is eligible and operationally usable. This is a conditional accounting proposition.
The third proposition is that timing can matter independently of the final tax cost. A recoverable amount can still require financing while it remains unavailable. The fourth is that the value of formal transaction records depends on how they are used by customers, suppliers and lenders. Registration by itself does not guarantee additional sales or cheaper credit.
These propositions are not statistical hypotheses tested with primary observations in this project. They provide a clear route from the literature to the analysis and identify what future research would need to measure. Each proposition could be challenged with suitable evidence. For example, a firm-specific cost study might show that variable transaction work dominates fixed fees in a particular sector. The report’s framework is intended to accommodate such differences rather than conceal them behind a predetermined conclusion.

# Chapter 3 — Research Methodology
## 3.1 Research design and unit of analysis
The study follows the descriptive and analytical design specified in the synopsis. The descriptive component explains GST’s structure, relevant enterprise categories and published indicators. The analytical component compares the meaning of those indicators, examines business mechanisms and develops reasoned recommendations. All research evidence comes from secondary sources.
The primary unit of analysis is the published document or statistical series. There is no respondent sample and no questionnaire administered by the student. The use of published survey results does not convert the project into primary research: the original investigators collected those responses, while this report evaluates their published findings.
The design is appropriate to the objectives because the project seeks to understand a policy framework and its implications across several dimensions. A documentary study can combine legal, statistical and research material without claiming access to confidential firm records. It can also identify gaps that would be obscured by a small convenience sample presented as nationally representative.
The design has limits. It is unable to estimate a causal average treatment effect or verify the experience of an individual enterprise. Accordingly, the language of the report distinguishes “the source reports”, “the calculation shows” and “the analysis suggests”. These phrases indicate different evidential foundations. Findings based on actual published data are kept separate from numerical examples prepared to explain a mechanism. No assumed number is entered into an official dataset, and no hypothetical case is described as a business that was visited or interviewed.
===
## 3.2 Selection of documentary evidence
Sources were selected purposively for relevance to the five objectives and for traceability to the original publisher. This is documentary sampling, not random sampling of enterprises. Inclusion depends on whether the source identifies its subject, period and measurement clearly enough to support the intended claim.
~ Table 3.1. Source inclusion and exclusion rules.
| Include | Exclude or restrict |
| Official legislation and administrative guidance | Undated summaries treated as current law |
| Government series with defined units and periods | Unlabelled charts without an identifiable source |
| Identifiable original research publications | References whose publication details cannot be verified |
| Original publisher’s survey findings | Survey percentages with an unknown denominator |
| Historical material clearly labelled by period | Old rules presented as current requirements |
| Transparent calculations from cited inputs | Invented respondents, interviews or observed cases |
A source can be included for one purpose and restricted for another. A historical circular helps explain the transition environment but does not describe today’s relief arrangements. A current classification document clarifies definitions but should not be used to reclassify an older time series without the underlying data.
The evidence set was designed to be manageable and auditable. It does not claim to include every study of GST. The final reference list records sources actually used, while the data appendices preserve key numbers and formulas. A reader can therefore retrace the main analytical steps without relying on an unspecified collection of websites or on inaccessible field notes.
===
## 3.3 Collection and extraction procedure
The documentary procedure begins with the research question, then selects an appropriate source type. Official data are used for national indicators; legislation and guidance establish the framework; research and published surveys provide analysis and reported experience. Each item is checked for the period and population to which it applies.
!FIG research_flow|Figure 3.1. Documentary research workflow.|Source: Procedure used to organise this secondary-data project.
For numerical material, the extraction record contains the variable, value, unit, year, population and source location. Gross revenue and net revenue are stored as separate variables. A provisional flag is retained when the publisher identifies the figures as provisional. Percentage shares retain their original denominators rather than being relabelled for convenience.
After extraction, calculations are performed using the reported inputs. Derived values are marked as calculations, with the formula shown in the appendix. Rounding is applied at presentation rather than before computation. A chart uses the same numbers as its accompanying table. The final interpretive step asks whether the figure answers the research question directly or merely provides background. This prevents a descriptive statistic from becoming an unsupported claim about causation.

## 3.4 Operational definitions
Clear definitions are needed because terms such as burden, benefit and performance can refer to several different measurements. The project uses the following definitions to keep the analysis consistent across chapters.
~ Table 3.2. Variables and operational definitions.
| Term | Definition used | Measurement boundary |
| Compliance cost | Resources used to meet tax-related information and process requirements | Distinguish cash fees from time and shared costs |
| Eligible ITC | Credit available under relevant legal conditions | Not every purchase tax is creditable |
| Cash-flow timing gap | Period in which a specified tax-related outflow precedes usable recovery or associated receipt | Not the firm’s entire working-capital need |
| Cost intensity | Specified annual compliance cost divided by annual turnover | Illustrative here; not an observed national ratio |
| Gross GST revenue | Collection before the refunds shown in the official release | National total includes defined components |
| Net GST revenue | Gross collection less the stated refunds | Do not compare with gross figures as if identical |
| Reported sentiment | Survey respondents’ published assessment | Opinion within the original survey coverage |
| SME performance | Operational or financial outcomes of the enterprise | No representative profit estimate is available here |
These definitions reduce ambiguity in the discussion. In particular, a higher tax payment is not automatically a higher economic burden, since turnover, credits and the treatment of the transaction may have changed. Equally, a credit balance is not automatically cash available for payroll. Each measure is interpreted according to what it records and what it omits.

## 3.5 Analytical techniques
The project uses descriptive comparison, thematic synthesis and transparent scenario arithmetic. These techniques fit the available evidence. Statistical significance tests are not reported because the report does not possess a suitable original sample or a common micro-level dataset for estimation.
~ Table 3.3. Analytical tools and limits.
| Tool | Application | Limit |
| Percentage change | Compare two annual revenue observations | Nominal change is not a causal effect |
| Percentage-point difference | Compare shares or sentiment percentages | Different from percentage growth |
| Ratio analysis | Relate an assumed fixed cost to turnover | Scenario, not an industry estimate |
| Sensitivity calculation | Vary delay and financing rate | Results depend on explicit assumptions |
| Thematic comparison | Organise compliance, liquidity and trade mechanisms | Does not establish prevalence |
| Triangulation | Compare sources with different evidential roles | Agreement alone does not remove shared bias |
The percentage-change formula is: (new value − old value) ÷ old value × 100. A percentage-point difference is simply the newer percentage minus the older percentage. These measures answer different questions and are labelled accordingly. An increase from 20% to 25% is five percentage points, even though the relative increase is 25%.
Thematic synthesis connects evidence to an objective and identifies any missing link. A rule can establish that a procedure exists, for instance, while a time-use study would be needed to quantify its burden. The analysis explicitly records this gap rather than manufacturing an estimate to make the argument appear more complete.
===
## 3.6 Reliability, validity and comparability
Reliability concerns whether the report’s extraction and calculations can be repeated. To support it, numerical tables cite their original release, use consistent units and show calculations where needed. The same input data feed the text and charts. This makes transcription errors easier to identify and prevents a visual from silently using a different definition.
Validity concerns whether a measure represents the concept being discussed. Gross national collections are a valid measure of the collection total defined by the publisher. They are not a valid direct measure of SME profitability. A survey sentiment percentage measures the response reported by that survey; it is not a substitute for audited changes in margins or productivity.
Comparability requires attention to time and population. Enterprise classification changed in 2025. Administrative coverage can expand as registration systems reach more units. Published survey editions can contain different respondent groups. The report retains these qualifications and does not combine all available numbers into a single trend merely because they relate broadly to GST or MSMEs.
Triangulation provides a further check. An administrative rule, a research paper and a published business survey can illuminate different parts of the same problem. However, agreement among sources is not proof of causality, especially if they rely on the same underlying information. The objective is to improve the interpretation, not to count citations as votes. A conclusion is kept narrow when the supporting evidence is narrow, even if a broader statement would be more dramatic.
===
## 3.7 Research integrity and secondary survey evidence
The report uses only secondary research evidence, in accordance with the synopsis and the student’s instruction. No survey was administered, no respondent identities were created and no interview quotations were invented. Published survey findings are identified by the original investigator, year, sample description and available limitations.
The distinction also governs the appendices. They contain a source register, extracted observations, calculation notes and guidance for interpreting published survey percentages. They do not contain completed questionnaires pretending to represent fieldwork. Numerical teaching examples within the analysis are labelled hypothetical and are excluded from the empirical findings.
Citation serves two purposes: attribution and verification. It acknowledges the origin of ideas or data while allowing a reader to locate the supporting material. The report paraphrases findings in its own analytical context and avoids reproducing large passages. When a cited source supports only a limited claim, subsequent interpretation is identified as the report’s reasoning rather than attributed to the source.
The same principle applies to personal and institutional documents. A supplied synopsis does not establish approval, and a blank signature line does not establish supervision. The certificate is therefore an unsigned completion template. The student and guide must verify the final work and attach authentic approval materials before submission. This is a practical distinction between preparing a research document and certifying that all university processes have been completed; the latter cannot be inferred from the information supplied.

## 3.8 Limitations and research boundaries
The first limitation is the absence of representative enterprise-level observations. The report cannot quantify how many SMEs experienced a particular problem or estimate an average change in profitability caused by GST. It can identify documented issues and explain their mechanisms, but prevalence and magnitude require additional evidence.
Second, official aggregate series use populations broader than the title’s everyday use of “SMEs”. MSME figures include micro enterprises, while national GST collections include taxpayers beyond the MSME sector. These sources are useful context but do not create a direct statistical link between the two populations. No ratio of national GST revenue to MSME output is used as an estimate of the sector’s tax burden.
Third, source periods differ. Some research concerns the transition, some covers pre-GST VAT, and later surveys reflect a more mature system. Policy changes after FY 2024–25 are discussed separately. The report cannot isolate every change in rules or reconstruct the exact legal position of every transaction over the entire period.
Fourth, published surveys may reflect selection, response and recall biases. Their findings are retained as reported experience within the source’s coverage. Finally, the worked cases assume particular costs, credit conditions and delays. They demonstrate relationships but do not supply missing empirical observations. Recognising these limits does not remove the usefulness of the study; it defines its contribution as a careful synthesis and a practical analytical framework, rather than a claim to have measured the reform’s total national effect.
